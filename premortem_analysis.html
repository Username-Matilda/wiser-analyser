Pre-mortem Analysis

Imagine failure, then prevent it

What this tool does: A pre-mortem asks you to imagine that your project has already failed, then work backwards to understand why. This mental shift—treating failure as an established fact rather than a mere possibility—bypasses our natural optimism bias and surfaces risks we'd otherwise overlook.

The research: Studies show that "prospective hindsight" (imagining an event has already occurred) increases our ability to identify reasons for outcomes by around 30%. By mentally time-traveling to a future where things went wrong, you access insights that forward-looking risk assessment misses.

What you'll do:

  • Describe your initiative and declare its failure as already having happened
  • Brainstorm all the reasons it might have failed—be specific and imaginative
  • Map how these causes connect, build a causal chain, and design prevention strategies with early warning indicators

Example: Research Grant Application (click to expand)

Initiative: "Submit a $500K research grant proposal to fund our new lab's first major project."

Failure statement: "It's 8 months from now. Our grant application was rejected. We're scrambling for alternative funding and the team's morale has taken a hit."

Some causes of failure:

  • The budget was too ambitious for a first-time applicant from a new lab
  • We didn't get feedback from anyone who'd served on this specific panel before
  • The preliminary data section was thin because we rushed the pilot study
  • A key competitor published similar work 2 months before our submission deadline
Notice the specificity: "too ambitious for a first-time applicant" is more actionable than just "budget too high." "Anyone who'd served on this specific panel" reveals a concrete gap we can fill. Specificity turns vague worries into addressable risks.

From causes to interventions: Once you've brainstormed causes, the path to prevention often becomes clear. "Budget too ambitious for first-time applicant" → research typical first-grant amounts, talk to program officers, start more modest. "No panel-experienced reviewers" → actively seek out former panelists for feedback. Each specific cause suggests its own remedy.

Running this as a team workshop? (click to expand)

Pre-mortems are especially powerful in groups because they give permission to voice concerns that might otherwise feel disloyal or pessimistic.

  • Round 1 — Silent brainstorming: Have everyone write causes individually (5-10 min). This prevents groupthink and ensures quieter voices contribute.
  • Sharing: Go around the room, one cause per person, until all are shared. No critique yet—just collect.
  • Round 2 — Build on each other (optional): After hearing everyone's ideas, do another 5 min of individual brainstorming. People often find that others' causes spark new insights.
  • Cluster & prioritize: Group related causes, then vote on which feel most likely or most dangerous.
  • Suggested timing: 10 min individual, 15 min sharing, 5 min second round, 15 min clustering, 20 min interventions
For sensitive projects with hard-to-model social dynamics: Creatively-inclined teams might try Theatre of the Oppressed-style role playing—acting out scenarios where things go wrong to tap into intuitions that analytical thinking misses. This "prehearsal" technique (see FoAM's work on experiential futures) can surface interpersonal dynamics, power imbalances, or motivational failures that are difficult to articulate abstractly.

Step 1: The Initiative

Pending

What project, decision, or initiative are you analyzing? Be specific about scope, timeline, and what success would look like. The more concrete your description, the more useful your failure analysis will be.

Step 2: The Failure Statement

Pending

Now make the mental leap: your initiative has failed. State this as a fact, not a possibility. Include a specific timeframe and describe what failure looks like—be visceral about the consequences.

The more real you make this feel, the better your subsequent analysis will be. Don't hedge with "might have" or "could have"—state it as fact.

Step 3: Brainstorm Causes

Pending

Given that the failure happened, why? Generate as many causes as possible without filtering. Specific, concrete causes are more useful than vague ones. Include both things within your control and external factors.

Prompts to surface hidden failure modes

Use these lenses to ensure you're not missing important categories of risk:

People & Team

Key person leaves or falls seriously ill, team hit by illness (e.g., severe COVID wave), burnout, skill gaps, miscommunication, conflicting priorities, knowledge silos

External Forces

Partners, funders, regulators, market shifts, competitors' moves, adversaries who benefit from your failure, environmental or political changes, supply chain disruptions

Timing & Sequencing

Delays that cascade, dependencies that slip, seasonal factors, coordination failures across workstreams

Resources & Budget

Cost overruns, funding gaps, equipment failures, supply chain issues, opportunity costs of this work

Assumptions & Blind Spots

Beliefs we haven't tested, things we're taking for granted, areas where we lack expertise, unknown unknowns

Reference Classes

How have similar projects failed? What are the essential features of this project—the critical elements that would bring down the whole thing if they fail? Consider using the Reference Class Forecasting tool to calibrate.

Motivation & Commitment

Enthusiasm fading over time, stakeholder priorities shifting, incentive misalignment, scope creep eroding focus

Complexity & Integration

Components that don't work together, handoff failures, emergent problems from system interactions, edge cases

Causal Chain: Work Backward from Failure

Starting from the moment of failure, work backwards through time. What happened immediately before failure? What led to that? Keep going until you reach the present. Flag any key uncertainties—points where you're genuinely unsure what will happen.

What to Do With This Analysis

  • Make prevention strategies actionable: Each strategy needs an owner, a deadline, and a way to verify it's been implemented. Add these to your project plan.
  • Set up monitoring: Put your early warning indicators somewhere you'll actually see them—a dashboard, regular check-in agenda, or calendar reminders.
  • Share with your team: If you did this individually, discuss findings with collaborators. If you did it as a group, circulate the summary.
  • Get external review: Use "Copy Report" to share with Claude or colleagues for a critical review. Fresh eyes often spot gaps.
  • Revisit at milestones: As circumstances change, so do your failure modes. Schedule a refresh at major project milestones.
