Key Assumptions Check

Surface the hidden beliefs your analysis depends on

Every conclusion rests on premises — some explicit, many invisible. This tool helps you find the shaky load-bearing assumptions: beliefs that are critical to your hypothesis, poorly supported by evidence, or difficult to test. These are where your analysis is most vulnerable.

The goal isn't to prove you're right. It's to discover where you might be wrong before reality teaches you the hard way.

This exercise works solo or as a team.

Before you proceed, ask yourself honestly:

Are you actually committed to uncovering things that might cause you to change your mind? Are you too attached to your current viewpoint or trajectory — such that you might rationalise certain assumptions as more solid or less load-bearing than they really are?

This tool is a compass, not a rubber stamp. If you're not willing to question approximately everything, it won't serve you. Don't use it to give yourself undue confidence.

💡 Why This Matters: The Maginot Line

In the 1930s, France built the most sophisticated defensive fortifications in history. The assumption? Germany would attack through the same routes as WWI. French planners were brilliant, methodical, and catastrophically wrong. They never stress-tested their core assumption: that the Ardennes forest was "impenetrable" to tanks. In May 1940, German armour punched through those "impassable" woods in days. The assumption wasn't examined because it felt obvious — precisely the kind of belief this tool is designed to surface.

💡 When Good Intentions Rest on Unchecked Assumptions

OpenAI was founded with the explicit goal of ensuring AI development would be safe and beneficial. Yet their entry into the market arguably accelerated AI development and intensified arms-race dynamics — potentially worsening the risks they aimed to mitigate. What assumptions might the founding team have needed to examine? Perhaps: "Being at the frontier gives us more safety influence than staying out"; "We can maintain our safety-first culture under competitive pressure"; "Open publication helps safety more than it helps capabilities." Each seemed reasonable. Together, they may have produced the opposite of the intended outcome.

Your Hypothesis or Analytic Line

State clearly what you currently believe to be true. This is the structure we'll examine for hidden supports.

Tip: Specificity matters. "Our area of research is valuable" hides assumptions in vague wording. Better: "The specific mechanistic interpretability work I'm doing on [X] is, all else being equal, my best focus area given my goal of [Y]." The more precise your statement, the harder for shaky assumptions to hide in ambiguous phrasing.

Questions to Deepen Your Analysis

  • What would it take to disprove your hypothesis? What evidence would change your mind?
  • Have any of these assumptions been true historically but may be changing?
  • Who would disagree with your hypothesis, and what's their strongest argument?
  • If your most critical assumption turns out to be wrong, what's your plan B?
  • Which assumptions can you test quickly and cheaply?
  • What's the base rate? How often do hypotheses like this turn out to be correct?

Before You List Assumptions: Excavation Prompts

Assumptions hide in plain sight. These prompts help you dig deeper than surface-level reasoning.

🎭 The Devil's Advocate

If you had to argue against your hypothesis in a debate, what would be your strongest three points? The assumptions you'd attack are likely your weakest.

👶 The Five-Year-Old Test

Explain your hypothesis as if to a curious child who keeps asking "but why?" and "how do you know?" The places where you say "it just is" or "everyone knows" are hidden assumptions.

🔬 The Evidence Audit

For each belief: Is your evidence direct observation, trusted testimony, logical inference, or gut feeling? Testimony from whom? What's their track record? Could they be wrong together?

⏰ The Time Traveller

Imagine someone from 2050 looking back at your hypothesis. What would they find quaint, naive, or obviously wrong? What "obvious truths" of today might be tomorrow's misconceptions?

🌊 The Consensus Trap

Is your confidence partly based on "everyone seems to think this"? Consensus isn't evidence of truth. Experts in a field often share the same blind spots. What if the herd is wrong?

🔗 The Dependency Chain

Your hypothesis probably depends on A, which depends on B, which depends on C. Map the full chain. Uncertainty compounds: if each link is 80% likely, a chain of 5 is only 33% likely to hold.

A note on epistemic humility: This tool exists because humans run on assumptions to function in an extremely complex, fast-moving world. It's not a character flaw — it's cognitive necessity. But it means nobody is immune to relying on unchecked assumptions, including experts, authorities, and you. The goal isn't to eliminate assumptions (impossible) but to know which ones you're betting on.

🚀 Where To Go From Here

You've mapped your assumptions. Now what?

  • Test the testable: For assumptions marked "Testable" — design a quick experiment or find disconfirming evidence. What's the fastest way to learn you're wrong?
  • Return to the Tool Navigator to find complementary analysis methods: Red Team Analysis to systematically attack your hypothesis; Monte Carlo Simulation if your hypothesis involves quantities or timelines and you want to see how uncertainty propagates; Reference Class Forecasting to check your predictions against base rates.
