Design Structure Matrix (DSM)

Reveal the hidden architecture of dependencies in complex systems.

Have a previous DSM? Load it to continue working.

Making the invisible visible: Every complex endeavour — a research programme, an organisational change, a policy campaign — involves elements that depend on each other. These dependencies are often implicit, residing in people's heads or buried in project plans. When they remain invisible, coordination failures feel like "politics" or "communication problems." Often, they're structural.

A Design Structure Matrix makes dependencies explicit and visual. By mapping what depends on what, you can identify bottlenecks (elements everything flows through), leverage points (where improvements cascade widely), circular dependencies (mutual dependencies that require iteration), natural clusters (elements that should be tightly coordinated), and sequencing opportunities (what can start when).

Confidence levels: Not all dependencies are equally certain. This tool lets you mark each dependency as high, medium, or low confidence — distinguishing structural realities from assumptions worth testing. Low-confidence dependencies are hypotheses about your system; treat them as candidates for scrutiny.

Reading the matrix: A mark in row X, column Y means "X depends on Y" — element X needs input, information, or output from element Y before it can proceed. Marks below the diagonal represent forward flow (earlier elements feeding later ones). Marks above the diagonal represent feedback loops (later elements feeding back to earlier ones) — these aren't necessarily problems, but they indicate iteration that needs explicit management.

How to use this tool: The worked example below shows the depth of strategic analysis possible with DSM. This interface provides foundational metrics — bottlenecks, leverage points, feedback loops, clusters, low-confidence dependencies. For deeper interpretation (counterfactual analysis, structural recommendations, theory-of-change scrutiny), use the Claude Analysis Template in the "What to Do With This Analysis" section. Arrows show dependency direction: A → B means "B depends on A" (A must provide something to B).

What the DSM reveals:

  • Hidden bottleneck: Executive Director has dependencies flowing in from almost every function (strategy input, relationship access, approval). If they're overloaded, everything stalls. The matrix makes visible what everyone feels but rarely discusses.
  • Critical circular dependency: Policy Research ↔ Government Relations. Policy needs to know what's politically feasible; GR needs policy content to advocate. This isn't a problem to "fix" — it's an iteration that requires explicit coordination protocols.
  • Natural clusters emerge: {Policy Research + Technical Advisors + Communications} share content dependencies. {Government Relations + Coalition Partners + ED} share relationship dependencies. Operations and Funders connect to both clusters but don't need tight coordination with each other.
  • Sequencing insight: Technical Advisors can begin evidence review early. Communications can draft messaging frameworks but can't finalise until Policy Research locks recommendations. Coalition Partners need GR intelligence before committing to joint advocacy.

The deeper insight: What feels like interpersonal friction between Policy Research and Government Relations has a structural explanation — they're in a feedback loop by design. Rather than treating it as a conflict to resolve, the organisation can design explicit iteration cycles: weekly syncs where Policy shares drafts and GR shares political feasibility assessments.

🧭 Before You Begin: Identifying Your Elements

The hardest part of DSM is choosing what to include. Too granular and the matrix becomes unwieldy; too coarse and you miss important dependencies. Start with 6-12 elements — you can always decompose further once you see the structure. Research consistently shows that gathering input from multiple perspectives — not just the project lead — produces more accurate matrices and surfaces dependencies that individuals miss.

For Process DSMs (tasks/activities):

  • What are the major deliverables or milestones?
  • What decisions must be made, and what information do they require?
  • Where do handoffs occur between people or teams?

For Organisational DSMs (teams/roles/people):

  • Who needs information from whom to do their work?
  • Where do approvals or sign-offs create dependencies?
  • Which groups must coordinate on shared resources or timing?

For System DSMs (components/modules):

  • What are the major subsystems or functional units?
  • What interfaces exist — data flows, shared resources, physical connections?
  • What can be changed independently vs. what requires coordinated changes?

Refinement heuristics: If two elements always have identical dependencies, consider merging them. If one element has very different internal dependencies, consider splitting it. If you're uncertain whether a dependency exists, mark it — you can always remove it after discussion.

Cross-tool support: If you're struggling to identify elements, try an Environmental Scan to map all actors and forces, or use Value-Focused Thinking to clarify what outcomes matter and work backward to the activities that produce them.

📐 Key Concepts

Clusters: Groups of elements that are densely connected to each other but sparsely connected to elements outside the group. Elements in the same cluster should typically be coordinated tightly — co-located, assigned to the same team, or given frequent sync points.

Modularity: A measure of how well-separated your clusters are. High modularity (60%+) means most dependencies stay within clusters, with clean interfaces between them. Low modularity means dependencies cross cluster boundaries frequently — which may indicate genuinely integrated work that resists modularisation.

Feedback loops: Dependencies that flow "backward" — from later elements to earlier ones (appearing above the diagonal). These create iteration cycles where neither element can finish without the other. Feedback loops aren't inherently bad, but they need explicit management through regular syncs and clear protocols.

Leverage points: Elements where improvements cascade widely through the system. An element with high leverage has many things depending on it (directly and indirectly). Investing in quality and speed at leverage points yields multiplied returns.

Confidence levels: Not all dependencies are equally certain. High-confidence dependencies are structural realities; low-confidence ones are hypotheses worth testing. Marking confidence explicitly turns your DSM into a map of what you know versus what you assume.

Step 3: Analyse Structure

The dashboard below updates automatically as you add dependencies. Use the buttons for deeper analysis:

Find Clusters: Identifies groups of tightly-connected elements. Most useful when your system might have natural module boundaries. Limitation: In highly interconnected systems (like most organisational DSMs), everything may end up in one cluster — which is itself useful information: it tells you there are no clean separation points.

Optimise Sequence: Reorders elements to push dependencies below the diagonal, minimising feedback loops. Limitation: Only useful for Process DSMs (task sequences) where you control the order of work. For organisational DSMs, you can't reorder stakeholders — the "sequence" is fixed by reality.

View Leverage Points: Shows which elements have the highest downstream impact — directly and indirectly. To spot leverage in the matrix: look for columns with many marks (many things depend on this element). High-leverage elements are where quality improvements cascade through your system.

What to Do With This Analysis

  • Bottlenecks: Elements with high fan-in (many things depend on them) are potential single points of failure. Consider adding redundancy, delegating, or redesigning to reduce centralisation.
  • Leverage points: Elements with high downstream impact are where small improvements cascade widely. Prioritise quality and speed in these elements — the returns multiply through the system.
  • Feedback loops: Circular dependencies aren't inherently bad — they often represent necessary iteration. But they need explicit management: regular syncs, clear protocols for when to iterate vs. when to lock decisions.
  • Clusters: Elements that cluster together have dense internal dependencies. They should be co-located, tightly coordinated, or treated as a module. Interfaces between clusters should be explicit and minimal.
  • Low-confidence dependencies: These are your hypotheses about how the system works. Test them deliberately — some "constraints" dissolve under scrutiny, revealing new degrees of freedom.

Counterfactual Thinking

For each critical element, ask the counterfactual question:

  • If this element failed or didn't exist, could the work proceed differently? Some apparently critical elements have workarounds; others are truly irreplaceable. This distinction matters for risk assessment and resource allocation.
  • If this dependency were removed, what would break — and what wouldn't? Dependencies marked as "low confidence" deserve this scrutiny. You may discover that some are self-imposed rather than structural.
  • What's the counterfactual impact of improving this element? For high-leverage elements, estimate: if this element were 20% faster or higher-quality, how much would that improve final outcomes?

Ecosystem Mapping: Beyond Your Organisation

DSM can map dependencies across organisations, not just within them. For advocacy coalitions, research collaborations, or movement-building:

  • External dependencies: Where does your theory of change require another organisation to succeed? These dependencies are often invisible until mapped explicitly.
  • Coordination interfaces: Which external relationships require active coordination vs. passive monitoring? High-traffic interfaces need explicit protocols.
  • Ecosystem bottlenecks: Is there a single organisation that many others depend on? This may be a fragility in your field, not just your project.

Phase Transitions

Dependencies change over time. Consider creating separate DSMs for different phases:

  • Research phase: Building evidence base, literature review, methodology development
  • Synthesis phase: Policy recommendations, stakeholder briefings, coalition alignment
  • Advocacy phase: Legislative engagement, media campaigns, public mobilisation

Revisit your DSM at phase transitions. The bottlenecks and leverage points shift as work progresses.

Using the Flowchart

The "Generate Flowchart" button produces Mermaid code — a text format that renders as a visual diagram. You can:

  • Paste into Claude: Ask Claude to render it as an artifact and help you analyse bottlenecks, critical paths, or suggest structural improvements.
  • Workshop with your team: Share the diagram in a meeting to discuss dependencies people may not have been aware of. Use it to surface hidden assumptions about who needs what from whom.
  • Create a Gantt chart: For Process DSMs, the dependency structure maps directly to project scheduling. Elements with no incoming dependencies can start immediately; the rest must wait for their inputs.
  • Document your system: The visual diagram serves as living documentation of your coordination structure — useful for onboarding, retrospectives, or restructuring discussions.
