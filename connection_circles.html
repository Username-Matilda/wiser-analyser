Connection Circles

"The behaviour of a system cannot be known just by knowing the elements of which the system is made." — Donella Meadows

Have a previous diagram? Load it to continue working, or load an example to see how this works.

Why map systems visually? Complex situations involve elements that influence each other in loops. A price increase reduces demand, which reduces revenue, which pressures margins, which pressures prices. Written lists hide these circular patterns. Visual connection diagrams reveal them — and reveal where to intervene.

Connection circles show reinforcing loops (where change amplifies itself — growth spirals, vicious cycles) and balancing loops (where change triggers counterforces — thermostats, market corrections). Neither type is inherently good or bad; both are present in most real systems, interacting in non-obvious ways.

The goal isn't a perfect map. It's a thinking tool. By making your mental model explicit, you expose it to scrutiny — yours and others'. Once you've mapped your system, use the Copy Prompt for Claude button at the bottom to get rigorous analysis and critique of your diagram.

📖 Example: The Hiring-Quality Spiral

Scenario: A growing startup noticing declining code quality

The team maps what they think is happening:

  • Growth Pressure → (+) → Hiring Speed: More growth demands faster hiring
  • Hiring Speed → (−) → Hiring Quality: Faster hiring reduces vetting thoroughness
  • Hiring Quality → (+) → Code Quality: Lower-quality hires produce lower-quality code
  • Code Quality → (+) → Development Speed: Poor code slows everyone down
  • Development Speed → (−) → Growth Pressure: Slower delivery creates more pressure

The insight: Mapping this reveals a vicious cycle. The "obvious" solution (hire faster) actually makes things worse. The high-leverage intervention is at Hiring Quality — slow down hiring, raise the bar, and the whole system improves.

🎯 Choosing the Right Elements

The quality of your system map depends on identifying elements that capture what actually matters. Our intuitive categories often miss what drives outcomes — surface similarity can mask structural differences, and vice versa.

Questions to help identify essential elements:

  • What varies? System elements are things that can increase or decrease (customer satisfaction, hiring speed, technical debt). If it can't vary, it's probably not an element.
  • What would an expert notice? Someone with deep domain knowledge often sees distinctions invisible to outsiders. "Team morale" might be better split into "psychological safety" and "workload stress."
  • What's actually driving outcomes? Surface-level categories often miss the structural forces underneath. "Communication problems" might really be "meeting frequency" or "decision authority clarity."
  • What would change if you intervened? If you can't imagine how an element would respond to intervention, it might be too abstract or poorly defined.
  • Are you conflating different things? "Company culture" might bundle together "risk tolerance," "feedback norms," and "hierarchy levels" — which behave differently and connect to different parts of the system.

Aim for 4-12 elements. Fewer risks missing key dynamics; more becomes hard to interpret. You can always revise as your understanding develops.

🔑 Key Concepts

Reinforcing connections (+): When A increases, B increases (or when A decreases, B decreases). These create growth or decline spirals. Shown in blue.

Balancing connections (−): When A increases, B decreases (or vice versa). These create stability or resistance to change. Shown in amber.

Feedback loops: Chains of connections that circle back to their starting point. A loop with an even number of balancing connections is reinforcing (amplifies change). A loop with an odd number of balancing connections is balancing (resists change).

Leverage points: Elements with many connections, especially outgoing ones. Changes here cascade widely through the system. Donella Meadows' hierarchy suggests the highest-leverage interventions change system goals or paradigms, not just parameters.

📋 What To Do With This Analysis

  • Challenge your map: Show it to others involved in the system. What connections would they add or dispute? What elements are missing?
  • Test assumptions: For each connection, ask: "How confident am I that this relationship exists? What evidence would change my mind?" Consider using the Key Assumptions Check tool to formalise this.
  • Identify interventions: Look at leverage points. What would happen if you changed them? Use the Premortem Analysis or Red Team Analysis to stress-test intervention ideas.
  • Map dependencies: For project or organisational systems, the Design Structure Matrix provides a complementary view focused on task/role dependencies.
  • Consider wider forces: Your system exists within larger systems. Use Outside-In Thinking to map the external constraints and forces acting on your system.

📚 Further Reading & Resources

Systems Thinking Foundations

Donella Meadows, Thinking in Systems: A Primer (2008) — The classic accessible introduction. Her essay "Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System" is essential reading and freely available online.

Peter Senge, The Fifth Discipline (1990) — Systems thinking applied to organisations. The archetypes chapter (Limits to Growth, Shifting the Burden, etc.) provides useful pattern-recognition templates.

Jay Forrester, Industrial Dynamics (1961) — The foundational text for system dynamics modelling. More technical than Meadows or Senge; relevant if you want to move from qualitative diagrams to quantitative simulations.

John Holland, Signals and Boundaries: Building Blocks for Complex Adaptive Systems (2012) — Explores how complex systems emerge from simple agents exchanging signals across boundaries. Holland (creator of genetic algorithms) provides a rigorous framework for understanding how adaptation, learning, and emergence arise from basic mechanisms. Particularly useful for thinking about systems that evolve over time.
