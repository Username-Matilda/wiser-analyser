Reference Class Forecasting

Learn from structurally similar situations to ground your expectations in reality

The core insight: When we forecast from the "inside view" — imagining how our specific situation will unfold — we tend to construct optimistic narratives. The "outside view" asks: what typically happens to endeavors like this one? Neither perspective is complete on its own. The inside view holds crucial specifics; the outside view provides grounding. This tool helps you integrate both.
One class, one run. Each run of this tool examines one coherent reference class — one structural framing of your situation. Most situations benefit from multiple framings: run this exercise several times with different classes, then synthesize the results. The divergence between framings is where insight lives.

Step 1: Define the Success State

Describe what "done" looks like — not as an activity, but as a completed state. Be specific about observable outcomes.

Why this matters: Vague goals produce vague forecasts. "Launch the website" invites optimism. "New website is live, handles 10k concurrent users, and user testing shows 40% improvement in task completion" forces clarity about what you're actually trying to achieve.

Step 2: Register Your Inside View

Before looking at base rates, record your intuitive estimate. This is your "inside view" — your mental simulation of how this specific situation will unfold.

Why record this first? Once you see base rates, your intuition will be anchored by them. Capturing your inside view beforehand lets you genuinely compare your instincts against historical patterns — and learn something about your own calibration.

Step 3: Define Your Reference Class

A reference class is a set of situations that share important structural features with yours. The key word is structural: features that actually drive outcomes, not surface-level similarities.

The common trap: It's natural to reach for obvious categories — "other projects by my organization" or "similar products in our industry." These aren't wrong, but they may miss structural factors that actually determine outcomes. A PhD thesis might share more structural DNA with "solo creative projects with distant deadlines and ambiguous success criteria" than with "academic work."

A bucket error occurs when we group things together that appear similar but differ in ways that matter for outcomes — or separate things that appear different but share crucial dynamics.

For example: "technology startups" might seem like a coherent reference class, but a B2B SaaS company and a hardware robotics company have wildly different risk profiles, capital requirements, and timeline dynamics. Conversely, "PhD completion" and "writing a novel" might seem very different but share structural features: solo work, distant deadlines, intrinsic motivation requirements, scope creep vulnerability.

The goal isn't to reject surface-level categories — they often do capture something real. The goal is to examine whether they capture what drives outcomes, and to complement them with classes that capture structural factors you might otherwise miss.

Collected Patterns

What's next? Run this exercise again with a different reference class. Your situation likely has multiple valid structural framings — "government IT projects," "social movements," "organizational change initiatives" might all illuminate different aspects of the same endeavor. The divergence between framings often reveals more than any single forecast.
The deeper value: You may find that the most valuable part of this exercise is noticing assumptions you hadn't examined — factors you'd have glossed over while designing a project or conceptualizing a problem space. The base rate numbers are useful, but the structured examination of your own thinking may be more useful still.
