The Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP)

AHP is a structured method for making decisions with multiple factors. Instead of trying to weigh everything at once, you compare criteria two at a time, asking: "Which of these two matters more, and by how much?"

📋 Example: Hiring a Software Developer

Your team needs to decide what matters most: Technical Skills, Communication, Cultural Fit, or Experience.

Instead of everyone arguing about percentages, each evaluator systematically compares pairs:

  • "Is Technical Skills more important than Communication? How much more?"
  • "Is Cultural Fit more important than Experience? How much more?"
  • ...and so on for all pairs

The math then converts these comparisons into consistent percentage weights.

Why Geometric Mean for Groups?

When multiple people provide comparisons, we need to combine them fairly. Geometric mean (multiplying values and taking the Nth root) is the mathematically correct choice because:

  • Preserves reciprocity: If person A says "X is 3× more important than Y", that implies "Y is ⅓ as important as X". Geometric mean maintains this relationship; arithmetic mean breaks it.
  • Reduces outlier impact: One extreme opinion doesn't dominate the group result.
  • Pareto optimal: The aggregated result respects each individual's preferences as much as mathematically possible.

💡 Staying Consistent (The Mental Algorithm)

Before rating, think through your rough priority order. Then check your comparisons follow this logic:

  • Transitivity: If you say A > B and B > C, then A should be > C
  • Proportionality: If A is "strongly" more important than B, and B is "moderately" more important than C, then A should be "very strongly" or "extremely" more important than C
  • Quick sanity check: Rank your criteria mentally (1st, 2nd, 3rd...), then verify your pairwise ratings match that ordering

Example Interpretation

Suppose you're hiring a developer and your results show: Quality = 40%, Risk = 30%, Cost = 20%, Time = 10%

This means your group has agreed that:

  • Quality evidence is ~2× more important than Cost – when evaluating candidates, portfolio/code samples should weigh roughly twice as heavily as salary expectations
  • Risk mitigation matters more than speed – it's worth taking extra time (3:1 ratio) to reduce the chance of a bad hire
  • Budget is a real constraint but not the primary driver – don't automatically eliminate candidates who cost more if they score highly on Quality and Risk
  • Time pressure is acknowledged but deprioritized – the group accepts that getting this right matters more than filling the role quickly

Key insight: These weights should guide how you weight evidence during your actual decision. If two candidates are close, the one who scores better on higher-weighted criteria should win.
