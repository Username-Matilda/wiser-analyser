What is Red Teaming? Red teaming is the practice of deliberately adopting an adversarial perspective to identify weaknesses in your plans, systems, or strategies. Rather than assuming things will work as intended, you actively try to break them—because in the real world, someone or something eventually will.

How this tool helps:

  • Profile your adversaries — understand who might attack, what they want, and what they're capable of
  • Identify attack vectors — systematically document ways your system could be compromised
  • Design mitigations — for each vulnerability, develop defenses that eliminate, detect, or contain damage
  • Track coverage — ensure every critical attack has a corresponding defense

Be specific in your descriptions. Include key uncertainties—what you're not sure about is often where vulnerabilities hide.

"Ordinary paranoia consists of thinking that random persons are out to get you. Security mindset consists of knowing that the world contains agents actively working to exploit your mistakes—but, more importantly, it's about defending against mistakes in your own assumptions rather than just against external adversaries."
— Adapted from Eliezer Yudkowsky, "Security Mindset and Ordinary Paranoia". See also: Bruce Schneier on security mindset.
Before you begin: Red teaming works best when you have a clear picture of what you're defending. Consider first using the Key Assumptions Check to surface hidden beliefs about your system, or Environmental Scan to map the threat landscape. An open mind is essential—the vulnerabilities that hurt most are the ones you didn't want to see.

🎭 Solo or Team Exercise

You can use this tool solo or step-by-step with collaborators assigned to different teams. The red team tradition comes from adversarial team exercises, and running it with multiple people often surfaces more blind spots. Here's how to structure the team version:

🔴 Red Team (Attackers)

Their job: Break everything. Identify attack vectors, find blind spots, chain vulnerabilities together. They should think like real adversaries—creative, patient, ruthless. Success = finding attacks the defenders missed.

🔵 Blue Team (Defenders)

Their job: Defend everything. Design mitigations, identify early warning signs, build resilient systems. They should think like security professionals—systematic, thorough, paranoid. Success = having a credible defense for every attack.

🟣 Purple Team (Integration)

Optional facilitator role. Coordinates between teams, ensures knowledge transfer, documents insights. After the exercise, helps both teams learn from each other. Particularly valuable for ongoing security improvement.

Basic exercise structure:
  1. Setup (15 min): Define the system under analysis. Both teams study it independently.
  2. Red Team phase (30-60 min): Red team identifies attack vectors without seeing Blue's defenses.
  3. Blue Team phase (30-60 min): Blue team designs mitigations for attacks they anticipate.
  4. Reveal & discuss (30 min): Compare notes. Which attacks did Blue anticipate? Which blindsided them?
  5. Iterate: Red team designs attacks against Blue's mitigations. Blue team adapts. Repeat until satisfied.

🎯 Red Team Principles

  • Assume competence: The adversary is smart, resourceful, and patient. They will find weaknesses you overlooked.
  • Think in chains: One vulnerability enables the next. A minor flaw can cascade into catastrophic failure.
  • No sacred cows: Every assumption is attackable. Every trust relationship can break. Question everything.
  • Hunt blind spots: The attacks that hurt most aren't the obvious ones—they're the threat classes you haven't been tracking at all. Prioritize finding what you've been missing over refining what you already see.
  • Seek the grain of truth: When a perspective feels uncomfortable or creates "wrinkles" in your nice, coherent worldview, that's often where truth hides. Coherence feels like truth, but it's often a red herring.
  • Document with care: Your red team documentation itself becomes an attack surface. Future defenders will be smarter than current you—but so will future attackers. Store securely, share judiciously, and consider what happens if this analysis is compromised.
  • Test your mitigations: A defense you haven't tested is a defense you've only imagined.

1. System Under Analysis

What system, strategy, or approach are you testing? Describe what it's meant to achieve and any key assumptions it relies on. Include uncertainties—what aspects are you least confident about?

2. Adversary Profile

Who or what might try to break this system? This could be a malicious actor, competitor, natural disaster, economic pressure, or even well-meaning people making mistakes. Be specific about their capabilities and what success looks like for them.

Multiple adversaries? You'll likely want to run this exercise separately for each distinct adversary profile. A broad sketch encompassing different adversary types will stop you modelling any of them with enough granularity to get solid results. If other profiles come to mind as you work, note them down and run additional rounds after this one.
Beyond rational agents: Don't just model what a cold, calculating adversary might do. Actually imagine being them. What's their worldview? Their values? Their fears and emotions, their social web? What does the world look like through their eyes? What cultural assumptions shape their reasoning, what past experiences inform their instincts? Adversaries aren't optimization algorithms—they're whole people (or complex systems) with their own coherent logic. Understanding that logic is how you predict what they'll actually do.

3. Attack Vectors

How could the adversary exploit weaknesses? Think creatively and ruthlessly. No attack is too obvious to document—sometimes the obvious attacks work precisely because defenders assume "no one would try that." Include your uncertainty about each attack's likelihood.

Seek the grain of truth: You may not consider an attack vector because your mental picture is too low-resolution and predicts that the world fills in the blanks smoothly, following the nice shape of what you're currently tracking. That's often a signal to look more closely. The vulnerabilities that sting to acknowledge because they run counter to your understanding, or hide in the gaps of your imperfect maps, can be all the harder to spot. A useful heuristic: if considering an attack creates "wrinkles" in your nice, coherent worldview—if it forces you to hold complexity that's uncomfortable—that might be where truth hides. Coherence feels like truth, but it's often a red herring. The territory doesn't smooth itself to fit your map.

4. Mitigations & Defenses

For each vulnerability, how do you strengthen the system? Good mitigations either eliminate the attack vector entirely, detect attacks early enough to respond, or contain damage if an attack succeeds.

Design mitigations thoughtfully: Resist jumping to the first obvious defense. Consider using Value-Focused Thinking to clarify what you're actually trying to protect before designing solutions. And remember: every mitigation can introduce new vulnerabilities. A backup system creates new attack surface. A monitoring tool can be compromised. A human checkpoint adds social engineering opportunities. Ask: "How might an adversary exploit this defense itself?"

📋 What To Do With This Analysis

  • Prioritize by severity: Focus first on critical and high-severity attacks that lack mitigations. These are your highest-risk gaps.
  • Test your mitigations: Don't just plan defenses—actually test them. Run tabletop exercises, simulations, or small experiments to see if they work.
  • Assign ownership: Each attack vector and mitigation should have someone responsible for monitoring and maintaining it.
  • Set review cadence: Adversaries evolve. Schedule regular reviews (quarterly?) to update your analysis as circumstances change.
  • Workshop further: Share this analysis with collaborators, domain experts, or AI assistants for additional perspectives. Fresh eyes often spot what you missed.
  • Run additional rounds: If you identified other adversary profiles during this exercise, run the tool again for each one.
  • Connect to other tools: Use Indicators of Change to monitor early warning signs, or Pre-mortem Analysis to imagine specific failure scenarios in detail.
