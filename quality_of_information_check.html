Quality of Information Check

Evaluate the substrate on which your knowledge rests.

The foundation of analysis: All conclusions rest on information. But information is not truth—it's a signal, filtered through sources with varying reliability. We rarely question the substrate: a claim is either believed or dismissed, with little examination of the path by which it reached us.

This tool makes explicit what remains implicit: How do we know what we claim to know?

A note on credibility: Track record can mislead. A source might be consistently "right" because they're genuinely insightful—or because they've echoed consensus that happened to be correct. The deeper question isn't just "Has this source been accurate?" but "Can you assess whether their underlying reasoning is sound?" Do they demonstrate genuine grasp of the domain, or are they pattern-matching conclusions to what others believe? Even top-rated experts and prestigious journals get it completely wrong often enough. Tetlock's forecasting research found that many confident experts perform barely better than chance—while "superforecasters" succeed through intellectual humility and constant self-correction, not superior access or credentials.

A deeper layer: Your assessment of any source's reliability is bounded by your own understanding. If you lack expertise in a domain, you may not be able to distinguish genuine insight from confident-sounding nonsense. The source might be wrong in ways you can't detect—or right for reasons you can't verify. This creates a recursion: you might seek help from others, but how do you know whose judgment to trust? You can't escape this entirely—at some point, every chain of verification terminates in judgment. But you can apply rigor to the question of who to trust: Does this person have a track record of being right in ways that weren't obvious at the time? Do they show their reasoning, or just their conclusions? Do they update when proven wrong, or defend their errors? Are they willing to say "I don't know"? Trust people who've earned it through demonstrated calibration and transparent reasoning—not credentials, confidence, or social proof.

📖 Worked Example: Evaluating Sources for a Strategic Decision

Scenario: Should we expand to a second site?

You're evaluating three information sources informing your decision:

  • Industry analyst report: Credibility medium (decent track record, but often echoes consensus); Bias medium (paid by clients who benefit from optimistic forecasts); Timeliness current; Corroboration unverified. Quality: 1.25 — Questionable. Useful for market sizing, but treat predictions skeptically.
  • Internal financial model: Credibility high (built by your team, assumptions transparent); Bias low (no external incentive); Timeliness current; Corroboration N/A (unique to you). Quality: 1.75 — High. But only as good as its assumptions—cross-check inputs.
  • Competitor's public statements: Credibility low (incentive to mislead); Bias high (strategic communications); Timeliness current; Corroboration contradicted by their actions. Quality: 0.5 — Unreliable. Useful only as a signal of what they want you to believe.

Insight: The "authoritative" analyst report is actually your weakest source. Your internal model—humble, transparent, yours—is strongest. The competitor's statements are information about their strategy, not truth about the market.

🧭 Dimensions to Consider

  • Credibility: Has the source been right before? How often wrong? Do they demonstrate genuine understanding or just pattern-match to consensus?
  • Bias & motivation: What does the source gain from this claim? Who funds them? What's their incentive structure?
  • Primary vs. secondary: Are they reporting what they observed directly, or what someone told them?
  • Timeliness: When was this information current? Has context shifted since then?
  • Corroboration: Do independent sources agree? Where do they diverge, and why?
  • Previously dismissed: Is there information you ignored because it contradicted your preferred narrative? Go find it.

What to Do With This Analysis

  • For high-quality sources: These form your evidential bedrock. But stay humble—even strong sources can be wrong. What would falsify their claims?
  • For questionable sources: Don't discard them, but weight them appropriately. What additional verification would raise your confidence? Can you find independent corroboration?
  • For unreliable sources: Ask what they're useful for. A biased source still tells you what that actor wants you to believe—which is itself information.
  • Cross-check your foundations: Use the Key Assumptions Check tool to see whether weak sources underpin critical assumptions in your analysis.
  • Calibrate your claims: If your information base is weaker than you'd like, consider the Reference Class Forecasting tool to anchor predictions in historical base rates rather than relying on potentially shaky sources.

📚 Further Reading

Philip Tetlock's Superforecasting (2015) — Explores how ordinary people can outpredict credentialed experts by practicing epistemic humility, updating beliefs incrementally, and distinguishing genuine insight from confident consensus-echoing.

Mellers, Tetlock & Arkes, "Forecasting tournaments, epistemic humility and attitude depolarization" (2019, Cognition) — Empirical research on the connection between forecasting skill and intellectual humility. Superforecasters aren't just more accurate—they're more open to revising their views.

Sherman Kent and CIA intelligence analysis — Kent, "the father of intelligence analysis," anticipated the dangers of confirmatory bias, the importance of allowing dissent, and the need for precision in probability estimates. Yet he also fell prey to scientific hubris. The Cuban Missile Crisis failure is instructive: after the CIA failed to predict Soviet missiles in Cuba, Kent argued their misestimate "got an ex post facto validation" because what they predicted would be "sound judgment on the part of the Politburo." In other words, they were right about what the Soviets should have done, just wrong about what they did do. This is the danger of reasoning from "what would be rational" rather than empirically testing your model. (See "Beacon and Warning: Sherman Kent, Scientific Hubris, and the CIA's Office of National Estimates," Texas National Security Review, 2018)
